Point analysis · warm-up

Every crime, every dot. Can you read it?

A Chicago-shaped city with made-up crime data. Work through the five steps and write what you notice on your handout.

Step 1: Crime in 2022

This map shows every reported crime in the city in 2022: incidents, one dot each.

You’re the new police chief. You have 5 extra patrol cars tonight. Where do you send them?

Take 60 seconds. Then try the buttons.

Step 2: Now you have 2023 too

The mayor asks: “Did crime get better or worse, and where?” Here are four ways to show two years of points. Try each one.

For each option, write on your handout: What can you see? and What can’t you see?

Step 3: Break it down by crime type

Each dot now shows what kind of crime it was. The chief wants to plan different responses for robbery and homicide.

On your handout: Where is each type centered? Which is more spread out? Does either one stretch in a direction? How sure are you?

Step 4: Add the police stations

The city has six police stations, A to F. A new robbery was just reported at the red ring.

Which station is closest? Make your guess.

Then discuss: Which part of the city is farthest from any station? Which station do you think handles the most crime? Could you tell just by looking?

Step 5: What are the problems?

You had every crime, every type and every station on a map. Why was it still hard to answer the chief and the mayor?

With your neighbor, list as many problems with point data as you can on your handout. Think about what the dots show, what they hide, and how they could mislead.

Go back to any step if it helps.

  • Too many dots: the map becomes one blob, so everywhere looks like a hotspot.
  • Dots hide each other: one dot can be 1 incident or 40 at the same address.
  • Hard to judge density: the eye can’t compare “a lot” with “a lot more.”
  • Zoom changes the story: zoomed out it’s a mass; zoomed in it looks spread out.
  • Comparing years is hard: on top is a mess, side by side is guesswork, a table loses the “where.”
  • Too many types at once: eight colors become noise, and rare but serious crimes disappear.
  • Hard to compare types: you can’t tell by eye where each type is centered or how spread out it is.
  • Distance by eye is unreliable: two stations can look equally close when they aren’t.
  • Which station is busiest? You can’t count thousands of dots per station by looking.
  • Our eyes get fooled: we see patterns in random dots and miss real ones.
